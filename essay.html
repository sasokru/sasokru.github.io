The Geometry of Exclusion

Latent space and epistemic invisibility in medical AI — and what it means for the promise of equitable diagnostics.

Leuphana University  ·  Digital Media, Net Criticism
Download the full paper (with citations)

In the United States, hospitals long used a standard formula to estimate kidney function called the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate. For years it carried a built-in variable called race, and it quietly assigned Black patients higher kidney-function scores than their clinical situation justified. Its effect was concrete: later referrals and longer waits on transplant lists, while actually no doctor was making a racial decision, but the algorithm was doing it for them, and nobody questioned it, because it produced results.

Medical AI is usually introduced as the cure for exactly this kind of unequal treatment. It is supposed to be a technology that promises consistent, high-quality diagnosis regardless of where you live or who treats you. That promise is real. But the eGFR case raises a question that gets far less attention: whose body was the system actually built for?

To what extent does the latent space of medical AI render certain bodies epistemically invisible?

Data is never neutral

It is tempting to treat AI as pure mathematics, and therefore objective. But the mathematics only ever operate on the data they are given, and that data comes from somewhere specific. More than half of published clinical AI studies draw on data from just two countries. The patients most likely to appear are those with access to tertiary care at research hospitals, enrolled in studies, whose records can be used under the right consent frameworks. This isn't deliberate exclusion, as it's simply how research data is produced. But the effect is systematic: some populations are structurally present in the training data, and others are structurally absent.

Latent space as infrastructure

To see where exclusion enters the model, look at what happens between the input (e.g. a medical image) and the output (e.g. a diagnosis). That in-between is the latent space. When a model trains, it doesn't memorise images; it builds a compressed internal representation of the patterns across them. That representation is effectively the model's theory of the body and what counts as a meaningful difference.

And a theory is only as broad as the evidence it came from as made concrete by Schön and colleagues (2022). Training generative models on a large set of thoracic CT scans, they recovered the model's entire working vocabulary for the human thorax in eight directions: width, height, size, rotation, position, thickness, breast size. Anything that can be described by the model is a point along those eight axes. Anything that cannot be found along them cannot really be processed at all.

The geometry of absence

Training images don't vanish into the model because each one leaves an imprint, making the region of the space around it denser and more structured. Picture footpaths across open ground: the more a path is walked, the more defined it becomes, and the more the next walker naturally follows it. Training data walks the first paths.

So if a kind of body was never in the data, no path leads toward it. The space where it would sit stays structurally uninhabited. not blocked, just never built. When the model later meets such a patient, it still produces an output, often with high confidence, but from a region of its own representation it never actually learned. The problem ia that the space it thinks through was never shaped to hold them.

Hermeneutical injustice

Miranda Fricker's idea of hermeneutical injustice names a harm that comes not from individual bias but from a gap in the shared resources a community has for making sense of an experience. The latent space is exactly such a resource. Those eight geometric directions feel objective because they are geometry — but geometry learned from specific patients, in specific hospitals, at a specific time. A patient outside that vocabulary isn't merely harder to classify as they are rendered unintelligible, because the categories were never formed around bodies like theirs.

This doesn't stay in the architecture. Dermatology systems trained mostly on lighter skin have shown markedly worse melanoma detection on darker skin which is a gap that often went unreported. An ICU mortality model trained on open intensive-care data showed recall as low as 25% for Black patients relative to the overall population. The model was operating exactly within the parameters its data had set.

Trust, and how bias becomes invisible

At the point of care, these structural gaps turn into decisions. Studying endoscopists judging the same lesions with and without AI, Reverberi and colleagues (2022) measured how far physicians shifted toward the AI's recommendation which totaled an influence weight of about three. Clinicians weighted the machine's opinion roughly three times more than their own independent judgement, even when they believed they were reasoning for themselves.

And once trust in a tool is established, it is applied uniformly — to every patient, including those the model represents poorly. The disparities that begin in the geometry of the latent space are absorbed into practice, laundered through trust, and made structurally invisible.

Conclusion

The eGFR formula has since been revised. But as an example it shows how an instrument built with clinical good intentions can encode assumptions that produce unequal outcomes at scale and how those outcomes escape scrutiny precisely because they sit inside a trusted tool. Medical AI is no different as the harm begins in the geometry of the latent space, a structure shaped by the specific bodies in the training data and simply empty where they were absent.

None of this is an argument against medical AI. It's an argument for taking it seriously enough to build it better: demographic transparency in how datasets are documented, mandatory subgroup analysis in evaluation, and data collection that actually includes the people most affected. The latent space can only speak for all of us if it was built from all of us, which I see as not only an ethical demand but a mathematical one, since a model can only ever generalise what it has seen.
